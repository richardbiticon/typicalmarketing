A note on how I'd think about Typical

The towel is where we start. It probably isn't where the value is.

Most people will read Typical as a towel brand, and the obvious job would be to sell more towels more efficiently. I think that misreads what the founders have set up, and the difference matters because it decides where the money should go for the next couple of years. This is the thinking behind the plan.

Read on ↓
01

What's actually being built

The founders went looking for a category nobody had bothered to put a real brand on. Ask someone for their favourite towel brand and watch them stall. That blank is the opening, and it sits over most of the things people use at home every day, not just towels. So the way I read it, the real work isn't moving more units. It's becoming the name people reach for in a category that doesn't have one yet, and getting there before someone with a bigger balance sheet notices the same gap.

This is also a pattern one of the founders has run before. Take a dull, durable category, give it a point of view and a design language people actually want in their home, then widen out from there. The towel is the entry point. It earns the attention and the first customer. What we do with that attention is the part that decides how big this gets.

02

The one fact that shapes everything

A towel lasts for years. People don't reorder them the way they reorder shampoo, and that quietly breaks the usual direct-to-consumer model. The standard approach spends more to win a customer than the first order returns, then earns it back on the second and tenth purchase. That works for consumables. Here it doesn't, and if we judge Typical by repeat-purchase rate, the business will look smaller than it is and we'll talk ourselves out of spending to grow. That's a measurement problem, not a real ceiling.

The growth comes from two places the reorder model ignores. The first is how much someone buys in a single order: a full set of bath, hand, and face towels, a mat, a robe, instead of one bath towel. The second is how many of our categories they buy into as we add them. A customer is worth a bathroom today, and if we earn it, a bedroom and a kitchen later. On top of that, the product is one of the easier gifts to give, so a fair share of buyers will put us in someone else's home at no acquisition cost.

If we judge it on how often people come back

The numbers look thin, and the natural reaction is to stop spending.

Low repeat rate reads as low lifetime value, which reads as a hard limit on what we can pay to acquire someone, which means paid can't scale and the business looks capped. Every quarter turns into re-acquiring from scratch, usually with discounts that chip away at the brand. None of that is wrong arithmetic. It's just the wrong question for a durable product.

If we judge it on basket size and categories owned

The same business has room to grow, and brand spend starts to make sense.

Order value and category breadth do the work that reorders can't. A captured customer isn't one towel of margin, they're a set today and a future portfolio over time. Measured this way, paying properly to acquire someone, holding premium pricing, and investing in brand all read as reasonable rather than reckless. The arithmetic is the same. The lens decides whether you invest or retreat.

Running underneath all three · gifting A good towel is one of the easier gifts to buy for someone, which makes gifting a way to acquire customers at almost no cost, not a thing we only think about in December. Every gift puts the brand in a home it hadn't reached and hands us a likely future buyer. 04

Almost every credible home brand leads with bedding and treats towels as a line extension. Nobody has made the towel the hero with a real design point of view. 06

What actually protects this

The stretch patent is good for the story, but in textiles a patent is easy to design around and a good towel can be copied within a season. If we want to hold the category, the thing that defends it has to be built somewhere harder to copy than the product.

Won't hold on its own

The stretch patent
Provisional and narrow. Useful in the press and the pitch, not as a wall.
The product itself
Any contract manufacturer can reverse-engineer a great towel before long.
Being first to launch
Launching first counts for little. Being the first brand people think of counts for a lot.

Compounds over time

Being the name people think of
Becoming the answer to "what towel should I buy" before anyone else claims the empty category. This is the main thing marketing is for here.
A point of view people recognise
Consistent taste is genuinely hard to copy. The distinctiveness is the asset, not the spandex.
Distribution and the owned list
Shelf space at the right stockists and on Amazon, plus an email list that carries from one category to the next.
Why moving now matters

My honest read is that the window to convert the launch attention and the open category into something we own runs maybe twelve to eighteen months. After that a better-funded incumbent or a fast copycat can step into the same space. That's the case for spending on brand earlier than feels comfortable, while the lane is still empty, rather than waiting until the metrics are perfectly proven and the gap has closed.

08

What this needs from you

Two things, really. The first is agreement on the measurement question from section two, that we're building a brand and a portfolio rather than optimising towel reorders, because that decision quietly governs how much we're willing to spend and how we read every report afterward. The second is a ring-fenced budget for the first round of tests, so the early months can produce real evidence instead of guesses.

Give me ninety days and I'll come back with an honest read on the numbers, a set of channels that work, and a way to launch the next product that the company can run without me in the room. The detail of how I'd spend that time is in the dashboard.
